1099 Prep: What You Might Need to Send

Here's the two-sentence version of what 1099s are: they're the forms that tell a worker (or a worker's bank) how much money moved when there was no payroll system to report it. There are several flavors; the one tiny businesses most brush against is the 1099-NEC (nonemployee compensation) for services, and occasionally the 1099-K they'll receive from a marketplace that processed their sales.

**This chapter explains the categories and the calendar. Whether you, in your tax situation, must send any given form for any given payment, is squarely: → taxes: ask a pro.**

The two hats you wear

Hat 1: you might send a 1099-NEC — if you paid a person for services

The mental model in one line: if you paid someone for services, track it; the rules decide whether a 1099 is due.

Your December job is just to have the facts ready, because the facts have a deadline and the facts are not pleasant to reconstruct in January:

  • Who you paid for services this year (contractors, freelancers, the person who fixed your laptop at the shop)
  • How much you paid each of them, by payment type (last chapter's categorizing already did the grouping)
  • Their W-9 — the simple form where a worker gives you their legal name, address, and tax ID. Collect the W-9 when you hire, not in January. Put "send me a W-9" into your hiring note template right now; this single habit eliminates the classic January scramble of emailing a person you haven't talked to since June.

A practical tracking tweak: in your categories or notes, mark payments that are services by an individual distinctly from software subscriptions or platform fees (see W-2 vs. contractor notes) — when the 1099 question arrives, you'll have a list, not an archaeology project.

Hat 2: you might receive a 1099-K (or 1099-MISC/NEC) — and should reconcile it against your own total

If you sell through a marketplace or collect card payments, the platform may send a 1099-K reflecting your gross processed volume. In late January, when it arrives:

  • Expect it to be gross — before their fees — not your net. Compare it to your gross sales column, not your profit.
  • It may include amounts that weren't "sales" in the ordinary sense (shipping collected, etc.). That mismatch is usually explainable, not wrong.
  • If a number looks wildly off, don't panic: platforms also have thresholds, filer quirks, and known lag behaviors. Check gross-vs-gross first, then platform help docs — and if it still looks wrong, contact them in writing, keep the reply, and note it in your archive.

The rule, calmly stated: the forms the platforms send are data points, and your reconciled books are the ground truth; a professional reconciles the two if they disagree. Where your own books are unreconciled, that's Chapter 1's job description.
