Categorizing a Year of Transactions

Your bank or card statement is a list of facts. Categories turn that list into meaning: "the business took in $X; it spent $Y of that on shipping, $Z on software, and $W on who-knows-what (now labeled)."

This is the biggest-feeling job of year-end, and it decomposes beautifully.

The naming system: keep it boring and few

A year of transactions for a tiny business fits comfortably into 8–12 categories. More than that and you're creating a taxonomy nobody will maintain, including future-you.

A workable starter set (adjust to your business):

Category What belongs in it
Sales incomeProduct/royalty/service income that hit your account
Refunds & returnsMoney you gave back (it reduces income; keep it visible, not "negative sales")
Shipping & postagePostage labels, packaging bought from anywhere
Supplies & inventoryThings you sell, or things you consume making the thing you sell
Software & subscriptionsThe SaaS tax you pay monthly
FeesPlatform/marketplace fees, payment processing, bank fees
Home/office costsIf applicable to your structure — category now, judgment later: → taxes: ask a pro
Professional servicesBookkeeper, designer, a person who fixed your widget
Travel & mileageFuel, parking — and see Mileage Totals
Equipment & toolsDurable gear over ~$100 — pricing/depreciation is → taxes: ask a pro
AdvertisingAds, boosted posts, business cards
OtherA real category, but a rate-limited one

The "Other" category: rate-limited by honesty

"Other" exists so nothing forces a bad label. The honest rule: if Other swells past about 5–10% of your spending, some of it deserves its own name. Rename mid-categorization, no ceremony required.

The process that actually works

1. Pull the year's transactions into one table

One row per transaction. Anything works: a spreadsheet you own, an export as CSV from your bank, or the report your software prints. Columns worth having:


date | description | amount (+in/−out) | category | notes

2. Sort by description (not by date!)

This is the whole psychological trick of the job. Sorted by date, 400 rows feel like a year of work. Sorted by description, you get runs of identical or near-identical text — "STRIPE PAYOUT" ×40, "EBAY FEE" ×30 — and you can categorize thirty rows at once with one glance to confirm they're really the same thing.

3. Attack the runs, then the stragglers

  • Assign categories to the repeated descriptions in their sleep.
  • Then handle the one-offs: short list, real thinking, done.
  • Vague descriptions (e.g., SQ *ABC): the retail counter code. Click through to the day in your records/calendar. If still unknown and small, "Other" plus a note of the date and your best guess. Unknown-but-small and rare is normal life.

4. Assign the ambiguous ones with a policy, not a mood

Write your ruling down so it's consistent:

"Professional membership → Professional services. Portfolio site hosting → Software. The convention's badge → Travel."

The point isn't which ruling you make; it's that you made one and it's documented. Decisions inside genuinely tax-shaped gray areas get the marker: → taxes: ask a pro.

5. Sanity-check the totals

Each category total should be less than total spending, obviously — and plausible. "Software" at $12,000 on a business that made $9,000 is not illegal, but it's suspicious, and suspicious usually means a misclicked category or a personal charge that wandered in. Personal charges should be pulled out of the business table entirely and noted separately; they don't get "invented categories," they get → taxes: ask a pro.

A checklist-friendly summary

When you close this chapter you should hold, on one screen, your year's income and spending in a handful of named buckets. That's the number this whole exercise exists to produce.
