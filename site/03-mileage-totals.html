Mileage Totals

Business miles are the one asset that evaporates at midnight December 31: whatever you didn't log, you don't have. This chapter is about reconstructing — and, more happily, about never needing to reconstruct again.

What this chapter is not: a ruling on what rate you may multiply miles by, or what makes a deduction valid. → taxes: ask a pro for both. What's below is pure record-keeping: totals, dates, and honest reconstructions.

The non-negotiables of any mileage record

Whether it's a paper notebook or an app, a real log row has, per trip:


date | start/end or an odometer reading | round-trip miles | purpose

"Purpose" can be one phrase. "Supplier run — goodwill drop-off + post office." Enough that October-you would remember, enough that a professional could place it.

Reconstructing a year you half-recorded

Everyone's first complete log is a bit of an archaeology dig. Dig politely, in this order:

  1. Recover the certain trips. Calendar invites, texted addresses, saved pins, map-app history (check its settings and retention before relying on it next year), the day's card charges for coffee while waiting at a tire shop. Landmarks in your year — a doctor's visit, a Ramadan week, the day you painted the studio — anchor fuzzy memory to real dates.
  1. Convert recurring trips into one formula. If you visited the same supplier the first Saturday of every month, that's 12 trips × a known round trip = one line in your math, cited to the calendar. Recurrence is your friend; it converts vague frequency into defensible count.
  1. Estimate the remainder with named methods. Where no records exist, a round-number trip ("about 40 miles, supplier run, monthly-ish") that you label as an estimate with the derivation ("12 such trips listed in calendar; assume 11 actually taken") is far better than either inventing fake precision or giving up. Honest estimates, marked as estimates.
  1. Stop at the edge of invention. Guesswork that starts filling in trips you can't corroborate at all isn't reconstruction; it's fiction, and it's where the → taxes: ask a pro marker earns its keep — a pro will tell you what estimate methods are acceptable for your situation.

The year-end total block

A one-paragraph summary for the archive:


Business miles, Jan 1–Dec 31, YEAR: NNNN
  - derived from: N fully logged trips, N recurring-trip formulas, N clearly labeled estimates
  - primary destinations: supplier, post office, events
  - log location: <notebook name / app name / spreadsheet filename>

Commutes and the classic trap

Driving between your home and the one place you always work is usually not business mileage — the log's job is only to count trips between business places. Boundaries here (home office rules, what even counts as "commuting" when your "office" is the kitchen table) are precisely the gray area marked: → taxes: ask a pro.

Next year's zero-effort plan (the actual payoff of this chapter)

  • Hang a mileage log page where you drop keys — or pin a log app to your phone's front page.
  • The log gets five seconds per trip, not December-morning-you getting five hours.
  • Whenever a trip recurs, add its formula to your notes file once; thereafter it's counted automatically.
  • Optional but luxurious: an odometer snapshot each January 1 and December 31 (two phone photos, stored with the archive). It makes the whole year mathematically checkable next year-end.

Done when: you have a single total miles number, written down, with its method notes, dated, and a plan attached for next year that involves approximately zero December effort.
