# What to Archive: Keep It, Prove It, Sleep on It

The year-end ritual ends with putting the year into a box — physical or digital — and never needing to open it except for good reasons. Done well, archiving is a fifteen-minute act of confidence, not a storage hoard.

## The golden rule of year-end filing

**One closed thing per year.** Not "business records," a shapeless pile that grows forever — but `Business Archive/2026/`, containing *the whole year's* books, statements, receipts, and reports. A shape is more portable: one folder, or one box, and everything about that year lives inside it forever.

## The rules about retention, honestly scoped

Exactly how long you must keep documents for tax purposes is a legal question (the federal answer involves the word "audit" and the phrase "six years" and then some fine print), so: **→ taxes: ask a pro** for the retention period for your situation. What this chapter gives you is the *workflow* that makes any retention period effortless.

Documents worth treating specially regardless of whatever the precise rule ends up being for you:

- **Returns** you actually filed (and their attachments)
- **Year-end totals** — income, expenses by category, mileage — the products of this whole guide
- **Permanent assets** — purchase records for the laptop long sale lives past the year it was bought
- **Anything in dispute** or with a dispute's ashes
- **LLC housekeeping** — your formation paperwork, any annual/lifetime-required filings and registered-agent info (state rules vary: **→ taxes: ask a pro**... in this case, actually, "ask the state" or that same pro)

## Digital archiving: the actual ten minutes

1. **PDF the things.** Bank and card statements, your categorization report, your mileage summary. PDFs age better than links (URLs die, portals reorder).
2. **Screenshot the dashboards that die.** Marketplace seller dashboards, platform fee reports for the year — anything that renders in a login and can't be re-extracted later deserves a PDF or screenshot *before* it scrolls away.
3. **One folder, named plainly:** `Business Archive/2026/`. Inside: `statements/`, `receipts/`, `reports/`, `1099-related/`, `mileage/`, `photos/` (mileage odometer shots, receipts photographed on the go).
4. **Copy it twice**, in two physical locations (a drive in a drawer + a cloud folder both count). One copy is zero copies by archival wisdom.
5. **Is anything in there sensitive?** (Full account numbers, customers' data.) Redact or store the truly sensitive pieces more deliberately; your archive should be a cupboard, not a display case.
6. **Write the index paragraph** — a dated, five-line note: what's in the folder, where the second copy lives, the retention question you decided on (and who you asked, **→ taxes: ask a pro**).

## Paper archiving: the actual five minutes

- Receipts that only exist on paper: photograph them into the `photos/` folder (well-lit, whole receipt visible), then staple or file the originals by month, or archive the shoebox, lightly sorted, if you prefer minimal effort.
- Filed mail that was meaningful this year (formation mail, state annual report confirmations, any dispute correspondence): goes into the year's folder too.
- The physical box gets the same number as the digital folder. `2026` matches `2026`.

## What you may actually toss now (relief section)

- **Old validated e-commerce claims** like "I'll need this screenshot" from a claim you resolved years ago without recourse
- Receipts for fully-depreciated coffee retailers you visited for vibes (that weren't business)
- Duplicates: three copies of the same statement
- **Before tossing anything financial**, apply the only real safety check: it's a*non*-record (not a return, not a payment, not IRS/state mail, not tieable to a year whose retention hasn't clearly elapsed) and not emotionally load-bearing (a very human category). Whatever retention rule you get from a pro, *that* rule governs the financial records; the toss list above is for the paper debris around them.

## The January-freedom payoff

Two copies of `Business Archive/2026/`, one index note, next-year folders pre-created (`Business Archive/2027/` with empty `statements/` `receipts/` ready). Twelve months of document-dropping, then next December becomes trivial again. That is the whole game, running smoothly.
